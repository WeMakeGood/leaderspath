## Synthesis

### Make Good Website: Consultation-First Strategy

Christopher frames the Make Good website around a single primary conversion goal: scheduling a free 30-minute initial consultation. The positioning is deliberately low-commitment for the prospective client: "it's, you know, just a an easy 30 minutes. Consultation. He'll probably go longer, but the idea is to make it feel like it's a low commit, you know, not a ton of commitment for the end user."

The consultation serves multiple strategic purposes beyond simple sales qualification. Christopher describes it as an opportunity to understand where a nonprofit organization is in their development, what's working, and what's not working. This diagnostic approach allows Make Good to assess organizational readiness for their consulting services while also providing immediate value through tips and guidance, even for organizations that may not be a fit as clients.

The qualification process reveals Christopher's philosophy about the type of work Make Good does and doesn't do. He explicitly distinguishes Make Good from traditional agencies: "if they're happy with where they're at, and they just want somebody to do marketing, there's a lot of people who can do that better, or who will do that for a set price. And they'll probably never be happy with it, but you know what? That's. That's what a lot of people think they need, is they need Group to go and handle, you know, an agency to go and handle their deliverables."

This reveals a fundamental positioning challenge: many nonprofits come looking for deliverables-focused agencies when what Make Good offers is capacity building and strategic transformation. Christopher acknowledges this misalignment directly, noting that if an organization isn't a good fit for Make Good's approach, the consultation becomes an opportunity to "point them to Great resources for how to do the work, that the the thing that they need."

### Content Architecture: Finding Yourself, Not Client Lists

Christopher articulates a clear philosophy about how the Make Good website should help prospective clients understand fit. Rather than leading with traditional client lists or logos, he prioritizes a "who we work with" section focused on organizational types and characteristics: "Rather than you know. Here's our client list. It's really, we're the type of organizations that we work with. And if people can find themselves in that? That's going to be great."

He dismisses traditional client list approaches with characteristic directness: "if we say we work with Netflix, we work with Amazon. We work with Motion Picture & Television Fund... We worked for the free Methodist Church that's kind of beside the point. Because nobody else. Only Netflix's Netflix, so it's kind of kind of a silly metric."

This "find yourself" approach extends to the "what we do" section, where Christopher grapples with communicating Make Good's value proposition to organizations that may be thinking in terms of specific service categories: "Sometimes that's really difficult for people to kind of. Understand is, you know, people are looking for. I'm looking for a social media company I'm looking for a marketing company. None of those things mean anything."

### Reality of Social Media and Digital Engagement

Christopher offers a particularly unvarnished perspective on social media effectiveness and client expectations. He describes how nonprofits often misunderstand what social media can accomplish, believing that "if I buy this product or the service, then that fixes my problem." His assessment of social media ROI is blunt: "Roi on social media, it's actually pretty bad. I'm generally speaking. Because social media is not designed. Help people get off of social media and be active. It's literally the opposite thing."

He uses a provocative analogy to illustrate the contradiction: "It's like saying. Yeah. Let me go to this, uh? Drug dealer's house, and I'm gonna find all these heroin addicts and hope for the best and, and, you know, try to get them to not be addicted to heroin because they can help me."

Despite this critique, he acknowledges the necessity of social media presence: "you can't not be there. Because it's oftentimes. A first step." The challenge, as he frames it, is understanding these platforms realistically and building proper engagement pathways that move beyond platform addiction patterns.

### AI Implementation as Capacity Building

Christopher positions Make Good's AI work not as a separate service offering but as integrated into their overall capacity-building approach. He emphasizes helping organizations "establish tools, guidelines, systems. Which includes all of our AI work" following "our ethical AI principles" to "Empower their organization" to fulfill their mission and goals.

This framing reveals how AI implementation is woven throughout Make Good's methodology rather than being positioned as a standalone service. The website needs to communicate this integration without falling into the trap of positioning Make Good as "an AI company" when they are fundamentally nonprofit capacity builders who happen to use AI as one methodology among many.

### Competitive Positioning Through History

Christopher identifies the "how we got here" page as particularly important: "This is a really important page because. It's our bonafidas really." He acknowledges the challenge of differentiation in a crowded market: "It's hard for. People to look at, you know, compare? House apples. When you're looking at Consultants? A lot of agencies say we use AI."

His approach is to tell the story of Make Good's evolution and pivots, demonstrating the benefits seen over their history. The implicit argument is that Make Good's track record and journey provide credibility that generic claims about AI capability cannot. This positions the history page not as typical "about us" content but as strategic evidence for why Make Good's approach differs from competitors who simply "sell it as a package."

### LeadersPath: Learning Community, Not LMS

Christopher is emphatic about how LeadersPath should be understood: "I want to be careful about saying, it's an LMS, because it's really. You know, we don't necessarily need to use an LMS for this." He distinguishes LeadersPath from traditional learning management systems, describing it instead as an "interaction interactive" learning experience focused on practical demonstration of AI capabilities.

The core pedagogical approach centers on experiential learning through chatbot interactions. Christopher describes a two-phase demonstration structure: "So the first lesson would be, hey, um, you know, talk to a bare LLM. Uh, Claude Ai chatbot, and this will be, you know, this will. All the chatbot will run through. The cloud API... they would say, oh, you know, write me a marketing piece about my org, and it would be really, you know, generic, and they'd be able to see, oh, this is the type of material that I'm getting."

The second phase demonstrates the impact of context: "Here's how to do it when you have. A context library in place. You know, a set of files that modules that explain who you are, what you do, why you know, values all that kind of stuff... the API Would Pull in this context documents." This contrast approach allows learners to experience the difference between baseline AI and contextualized AI implementations directly.

### Transparency as Teaching Tool

Christopher emphasizes making all lesson materials visible to learners: "We want to make sure that people know what do those files look like, so any files that are part that are used by the chatbot API? We want to make sure. Available for the user to look into and and to."

This transparency extends to skills (agentic processes): "if there's a skill that we use? An agentic skill that we've built that does sort of back-end processing and multi-phase. You know, focused development that they can access that skill. We want to make sure that they have access to that." The educational philosophy is that showing how systems work builds understanding and trust rather than hiding implementation details.

### Lesson Component Requirements

Christopher outlines specific elements that each lesson must include:
* Directions for how to proceed through the lesson
* Learning objectives ("what you're going to learn")
* Duration estimates ("how long it's going to take")
* Model specifications ("which API are we using, you know, because we might change to a specific model")
* References to additional resources ("other links... if There's a great article somewhere else, or an article that we wrote")
* Feedback mechanisms ("we'd love to go and hear what you think, you know, ask questions. Give us notes, tell us what worked. What? What didn't?")

The model specification requirement reflects technical precision: "we want to make sure it's clear this is what's going on here... we use. Opus for one Opus 4.5, and for another, we use sonnet, and for another, we use Haiku." This level of transparency about which Claude models are being used for different purposes serves educational goals while also demonstrating Make Good's technical sophistication.

### Course and Cohort Architecture

Christopher describes a hierarchy where courses serve as containers for related lessons: "courses then are going to be... archive Pages for specific courses, so of course. We'll then list all the lessons that are part of it in order. So, the order actually matters."

Importantly, while specific cohorts might focus on particular courses, access is not restricted by cohort: "any giving cohort might have. A course. That is, that's what they're working through. But honestly, we want to make all the courses available to everybody. If you signed up for a cohort? You know, we want to make that available."

The taxonomy structure is straightforward: "find a course. And then there's the course page. There's all the lessons click into the lesson, and you can use it if you're logged in." This suggests a browse-by-course approach rather than complex categorization systems.

### Technical Implementation Constraints

Christopher identifies Divi 5 as the platform: "We need to make sure that if you, you can only access that stuff with a particular type of user account. That we can do Baseline using [Divi 5]." The choice appears driven by existing familiarity and capability: "if you purchase a product, we can set you as a. As a customer as a user role, and then you know we can lock off specific content based off of user role, which is easy to. It's easy enough to do."

The access control strategy prioritizes built-in functionality over additional plugins: "I'd rather not pay for another plug-in if I don't have to." This pragmatic approach suggests using native Divi 5 capabilities for user role management and content restriction rather than introducing additional layers of complexity.

For the chatbot integration, Christopher indicates custom plugin development will be necessary: "there's the plug-in side of that which we'll build. Um, which would be the the chatbot part, but then there's the actual WordPress part." This custom development appears unavoidable given the specific requirements for API integration and the experiential learning approach that is central to LeadersPath's pedagogy.

Christopher expresses strong opposition to third-party LMS plugins: "having to go and plug, you know, specific content into specific templates for a third-party plugins, just such a pain in the butt. I hate having to do that. I've done that in the past, and it's more fun." This past experience clearly influences the decision to build custom solutions that can be owned and controlled rather than adapting to external plugin constraints.

### E-Commerce and Registration Systems

The cohort registration system needs to support multiple selection dimensions: "The shop is broken out into. You've got cohort types... people can sign up and then. Underneath those cohort types, you can send it for a specific date. The specific teacher and a specific language? At a specific time, or, you know, if you have a specific time zone that you want."

This multi-dimensional selection (type, date, teacher, language, time/timezone) suggests either a sophisticated product variation system or potentially a custom booking interface integrated with e-commerce. The need to accommodate these variables while maintaining usability will be a key technical challenge.

### Support and Transactional Systems

Christopher identifies the need for comprehensive support infrastructure: "We need support, so there's got to be a whole support section that gives people you know. Directions on how to use things." He distinguishes between pre-sale and post-purchase support: "people need to be logged in to actually get support. That's an important thing, but there's pre-sale support as well, so we will need a form for that."

For forms, he specifies WS Form as the tool: "we'll use WS form for that which it's awesome. I can tie into all the tools that we need to make sure that. Able to track, you know? We should be able to access this foreman, who shouldn't?" This suggests WS Form provides both the necessary functionality and integration capabilities for tracking and access control.

Transactional emails are flagged as requiring strategic thinking: "Obviously, there's all of the the transactional emails that we need to think through, so we need to outline, you know, what are the core transactional emails? Or any given action that somebody might have." This acknowledgment suggests email strategy is not fully developed yet and will need to be worked out during implementation.

### Cross-Site Strategic Relationship

Christopher describes how the two sites work together: "we want to highlight how cool these tools are on, make good. You know, there might be a whole like? The whole thing about why we do Leaders path and why Leader's path is important. Um, and then we want to pass people over to the leaders path site."

The strategic logic is that Make Good explains the LeadersPath initiative and directs people to it, while LeadersPath demonstrates capabilities that generate qualified leads for Make Good consulting services. The relationship is symbiotic but requires careful messaging to maintain the distinct identities of each brand while making their connection clear.

### Documentation Purpose and Urgency

Christopher concludes by articulating why this synthesis matters: "what we need, obviously, is, you know, how do we? We need to break all those elements out and. Make sure that we have clear guidelines for what each site needs, what each site doesn't. That's going to be part of this whole. This whole synthesis transcript synthesis is kind of making it clear to the website builder skill. This is what we need to go and build, um, because if we don't, it's just going to fumble around."

This reveals that the synthesis is not just documentation but serves as the primary instruction set for an AI-powered website building process. The clarity and completeness of this synthesis directly impacts the efficiency of the build process: "Process of attempting to figure out what I need rather than me telling it. This is what I need."

### WooCommerce Membership Plugin Options (Researched)

Based on 2025 market research, several alternatives to WooCommerce Members plugin exist for handling membership access control:

**Top-Tier Options:**
* **WooCommerce Memberships** (by SkyVerge/WooCommerce): $199/year - Official WooCommerce extension, seamless integration, unlimited membership plans, content dripping, member-only discounts, requires WooCommerce Subscriptions add-on for recurring billing
* **MemberPress**: $179.50/year (first year, renews at $359/year) - Comprehensive features, advanced reporting, content dripping, WooCommerce integration via bridge plugin, extensive customization options
* **Paid Memberships Pro**: Free core version with extensive features; paid add-ons available - Strong free version includes all payment gateways, complete content restriction, 34+ free add-ons, WooCommerce integration available

**Mid-Tier Alternatives:**
* **Restrict Content Pro**: Straightforward approach, beginner-friendly interface, flexible access rules, multiple payment integrations (PayPal, Stripe), competitive pricing
* **ProfilePress**: All-in-one membership solution, smooth WooCommerce integration, member directory feature, recurring and one-time subscriptions
* **MemberHub**: Balanced features and pricing, multi-level memberships, content restrictions, drip rules, analytics

**Budget-Friendly Options:**
* **ARMember**: Free version available with solid features including form builder, content restriction, customizable email notifications
* **Membership for WooCommerce** (by WP Swings): Free version with basic features, shortcode support, HPOS compatible

**Key Considerations:**
* Christopher's preference to "not pay for another plug-in" suggests starting with native Divi 5 user role capabilities
* If additional functionality needed beyond Divi 5 capabilities, Paid Memberships Pro's free version offers strong feature set without cost
* WooCommerce Memberships ($199/year) provides tightest integration but requires WooCommerce Subscriptions for recurring billing
* MemberPress offers most comprehensive feature set but at higher recurring cost ($359/year after first year)
